**[TURN 1 — SWE PUNDIT]**

Let us start with the headline statistic. On June 6th — eight days before this World Cup opener — Tunisia were beaten 5-0 by Belgium. Five goals. In 90 minutes. Belgium are a top-fifteen side in world football, but Sweden have Viktor Gyökeres and Alexander Isak — two strikers who, in a combined 37 international goals, have been doing exactly what Belgium's forwards did to that Tunisia defence.

Graham Potter has built something genuinely exciting in this Sweden side. The 3-4-1-2, with Gyökeres and Isak as the double-striker partnership, creates width through the wing-backs — Elanga pushing forward from the right, Ayari or Bergvall threading through the midfield. This is not a one-dimensional side missing Kulusevski. This is a cohesive tactical system under an elite Premier League coach.

Tunisia will set up deep. They always do. But their defensive shape conceded five goals to Belgium in their final preparation fixture. Against Gyökeres and Isak, in a World Cup atmosphere, that defensive record is simply not good enough.

**[TURN 1 — TUN PUNDIT]**

I want to give the Belgium result the weight it deserves — which is less than Sweden's pundit is assigning it. Tunisia qualified for this World Cup with what Goal.com called "defensive perfection." They topped their qualifying group. They drew with Mali at AFCON and beat them on penalties. The system — Skhiri and Khedira as the midfield double pivot, a compact 4-4-2 block — is built for exactly this kind of challenge.

June 6th against Belgium was a preparation friendly. Coaches experiment. Tunisia may have used it to test combinations and give minutes to fringe players. The result was alarming, yes. But the baseline for this team is what they showed across qualifying, not what they showed in one non-competitive fixture eight days ago.

Ellyes Skhiri is the heartbeat of this team. He dictates tempo and wins the second ball. With him and Khedira anchoring the midfield, the defensive shape that carried Tunisia through qualification will be restored. This is not going to be 5-0.

**[TURN 2 — SWE PUNDIT]**

The "it was just a friendly" argument has limits. Tunisia did not concede five because Lamouchi played the reserve goalkeeper and rotated six defenders. They conceded five because Belgium's forward movement found consistent gaps in their defensive shape — the exact movement Sweden's wing-backs in a 3-4-1-2 are designed to replicate.

Skhiri is a good player. But Potter's system creates overloads on both sides simultaneously. The wing-backs push high, pinning Tunisia's wide midfielders. Gyökeres holds the line, bringing Isak into play between the lines. Kulusevski's absence is real — but Elanga provides pace and directness from wide, and Ayari and Bergvall are increasingly established Premier League midfielders.

The qualifying record Tunisia point to came against CAF opponents in a different tactical environment. This is a World Cup Group F opener against a Sweden side managed by one of the most tactically sophisticated coaches at this tournament. The gap in quality is real.

**[TURN 2 — TUN PUNDIT]**

Sweden's case rests on the Belgium result and the quality of two strikers. I accept Gyökeres and Isak are elite. But football matches are won and lost in midfield, not in the striker positions alone. Skhiri and Khedira are explicitly designed to disrupt the build-up before it reaches those two men. If they do their job — and across qualifying, they consistently did — Gyökeres and Isak receive fewer clean chances than Sweden's pundit is projecting.

Furthermore, Lamouchi's omission of Sassi shows the coaching staff is willing to make hard calls for the good of this squad. That is not a sign of dysfunction — that is disciplined squad management. Tunisia's counter-attacking threat through wide areas remains intact.

My prediction accounts for Sweden's quality — I am not predicting an upset. But Tunisia will create at least one meaningful chance, will likely score, and will not simply capitulate like the Belgium result suggested they might.
